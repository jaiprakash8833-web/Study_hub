CLASS 9 • SCIENCE

Cell: The Building Blocks of Life

Chapter Notes · Definitions · Diagrams · Important Questions

All living organisms are made of cells. A cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life.

Learning Objectives

  • Explain cell theory and why cells are called the basic units of life.
  • Identify the main parts of plant and animal cells.
  • Describe the functions of cell organelles.
  • Compare plant, animal, prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells.
  • Understand diffusion and osmosis.

1. Discovery of the Cell

  • Robert Hooke (1665): Observed tiny box-like compartments in cork using a microscope and named them cells. He observed dead cell walls.
  • Antonie van Leeuwenhoek: Observed living microorganisms in water using a simple microscope.
  • Robert Brown (1831): Described the nucleus in plant cells.
  • Matthias Schleiden (1838): Proposed that all plants are made of cells.
  • Theodor Schwann (1839): Concluded that animals are also made of cells.
  • Rudolf Virchow (1855): Stated that new cells arise from pre-existing cells.

2. Cell Theory

  1. All living organisms are made up of one or more cells.
  2. The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life.
  3. All new cells arise from pre-existing cells through cell division.
Why is a cell called the basic unit of life?
Every organism is made of cells, and essential life processes occur inside cells or are coordinated by them.

3. Unicellular and Multicellular Organisms

Unicellular Organisms

An organism made of a single cell is called unicellular. That one cell performs all life functions.

Examples: Amoeba, Paramecium, bacteria and yeast.

Multicellular Organisms

Organisms made of many cells are multicellular. Their cells may become specialised for different functions.

Examples: Humans, dogs, birds and trees.

Levels of organisation: Cells → Tissues → Organs → Organ systems → Organism

4. Shape and Size of Cells

Cells vary in shape and size according to their functions. Most cells are microscopic.

  • Red blood cells: Disc-shaped; help carry oxygen.
  • Nerve cells: Long and branched; transmit messages.
  • Muscle cells: Elongated; help in contraction and movement.
  • Root hair cells: Have a long projection that increases surface area for absorbing water and minerals.

Cell size is often measured in micrometres (µm). One micrometre = 0.000001 metre.

5. Basic Structure of a Cell

A. Cell Membrane (Plasma Membrane)

  • Thin, flexible boundary surrounding the cell.
  • Separates cell contents from the external environment.
  • Selectively permeable: Allows some substances through while restricting others.
  • Controls movement of substances into and out of the cell.

B. Cytoplasm

  • Jelly-like material between the cell membrane and nucleus in a typical eukaryotic cell.
  • Contains organelles; many chemical reactions occur here.

C. Nucleus

  • Usually a prominent membrane-bound structure in eukaryotic cells.
  • Contains chromosomes made of DNA and proteins.
  • DNA carries hereditary information and helps control cell activities.
  • The nucleolus is involved in making components of ribosomes.
Important: Bacteria do not have a membrane-bound nucleus. Their DNA is found in a region called the nucleoid.

6. Animal Cell

An animal cell is a eukaryotic cell. Important parts include the cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus.

Simplified, not-to-scale animal cell diagram. Labelled parts are shown for revision.
  • Cell membrane: Controls exchange with surroundings.
  • Cytoplasm: Holds organelles; site of many reactions.
  • Nucleus: Contains DNA and regulates cell activities.
  • Mitochondria: Release usable energy during cellular respiration.
  • Endoplasmic reticulum: Helps make and transport proteins and lipids.
  • Golgi apparatus: Modifies, sorts and packages proteins and lipids.

7. Plant Cell

A plant cell has many structures found in animal cells, along with a cell wall, chloroplasts in photosynthetic cells and usually a large central vacuole.

Simplified plant cell diagram showing common structures.
  • Cell wall: Firm outer layer, mainly cellulose; provides support, protection and shape.
  • Cell membrane: Lies inside the cell wall and controls substance movement.
  • Large central vacuole: Stores cell sap and helps maintain firmness (turgidity).
  • Chloroplasts: Contain chlorophyll and capture light energy for photosynthesis.
  • Nucleus and cytoplasm: Present as in other eukaryotic cells.

10. Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Cells

Prokaryotic cells: Do not have a membrane-bound nucleus or membrane-bound organelles. DNA lies in a nucleoid region. Example: bacteria.

Eukaryotic cells: Have a membrane-bound nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. Plants, animals, fungi and protists are eukaryotes.

11. Movement of Substances Through the Cell Membrane

Diffusion

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, due to their random motion.

Example: Oxygen moves from blood into body cells where its concentration is lower.

Osmosis

Osmosis is the movement of water through a selectively permeable membrane from a dilute solution (higher water concentration) to a more concentrated solution (lower water concentration).

  • Hypotonic solution: More dilute than cell contents; water tends to enter the cell.
  • Hypertonic solution: More concentrated than cell contents; water tends to leave the cell.
  • Isotonic solution: Same effective concentration; no net movement of water.

Effects on Cells

  • An animal cell may swell and burst in a very dilute solution, or shrink in a concentrated solution.
  • A plant cell becomes firm (turgid) when water enters; its cell wall resists expansion.
  • In a concentrated solution, a plant cell may lose water and its membrane may pull away from the wall. This is called plasmolysis.

12. Levels of Organisation

Cells → Tissues → Organs → Organ systems → Organism

Example: Muscle cell → muscle tissue → heart → circulatory system → human being.

Specialisation allows different parts of a body to perform tasks efficiently.

13. Quick Revision

  • Cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life.
  • Robert Hooke named the compartments he saw in cork 'cells'.
  • Cell theory: organisms are made of cells; cells are the basic unit; new cells arise from existing cells.
  • Cell membrane is selectively permeable.
  • Nucleus contains DNA; mitochondria release energy; ribosomes make proteins.
  • Plant cells have a cell wall, chloroplasts in photosynthetic cells and usually a large central vacuole.
  • Diffusion moves particles down a concentration gradient; osmosis is water movement through a selectively permeable membrane.
  • Bacteria are prokaryotic; plant and animal cells are eukaryotic.

14. Important Questions with Answers

1. Why is a cell called the structural and functional unit of life?
All organisms are made of cells, and cells form their structures and carry out essential life processes.
2. Who discovered cells?
Robert Hooke observed box-like compartments in cork in 1665 using a microscope and named them cells.
3. State the three points of cell theory.
All living organisms are made of cells; the cell is the basic unit; new cells arise from pre-existing cells.
4. Give two differences between plant and animal cells.
Plant cells usually have a cell wall and a large central vacuole; animal cells lack a cell wall and usually have smaller vacuoles.
5. What is the function of the nucleus?
It contains most of the cell's DNA and helps regulate cell activities.
6. Why are mitochondria called the powerhouse?
They are the site of most aerobic respiration and produce much of the ATP used by cells.
7. What is the function of chloroplasts?
They contain chlorophyll and absorb light energy for photosynthesis.
8. Differentiate diffusion and osmosis.
Diffusion is net movement of particles from higher to lower concentration. Osmosis is water movement through a selectively permeable membrane from dilute to concentrated solution.
9. What happens when a plant cell is placed in concentrated salt solution?
Water leaves by osmosis; the cell contents shrink and the membrane may pull away from the wall. This is plasmolysis.
10. Differentiate prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells.
Prokaryotes lack a membrane-bound nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; eukaryotes have them.
